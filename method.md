Methods
Spinal cord tissue samples
Human spinal cord tissue samples were obtained from two sources: Sinopharm Gezhouba Central Hospital and Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University. Acquisition of human spinal cord samples was approved by Ethics Committee for Research Involving Humans, Institute of Biomedical Engineering, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College, IGDB Institutional Review Board, the Ethics Committee of Sinopharm Gezhouba Central Hospital, and the Ethics Committee of Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University Ethics Committee. All experiments were performed following the protocol guidelines. These samples were obtained with the consent of the patients, adhering strictly to legal and institutional ethical guidelines. Informed consent was acquired prior to the collection and the utilization for this study. These samples were dissected and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for further nuclei isolation or tissue section experiments.

Nucleus isolation and snMultiome data generation
Nuclei were isolated from fresh frozen human embryonic spinal cords adapt from a reported protocol2. Briefly, after dissected the spinal cord, placed the tissue in Dounce Homogenizer (Kontes Dounce Tissue Grinder). Added 500 mL of lysis buffer (0.32 M sucrose, 10 mM HEPES [pH 8.0], 5 mM CaCl2, 3 mM MgAc, 0.1 mM ETDA, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% Triton X-100) to Dounce Homogenizer and dounced gently with 10 strokes of pestle A and 10 strokes of pestle B, then mixed the lysate with 3 mL of sucrose buffer (0.32 M sucrose, 10 mM HEPES [pH 8.0], 5 mM CaCl2, 3 mM MgAc, 0.1 mM ETDA, 1 mM DTT) and filter with 40 μm strainer. Centrifuged the mixture at 3,200 x g for 5 min at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended with 1 mL PBS, then pelleted the nuclei by centrifuging at 600 x g for 5 min and resuspended in diluted nucleus buffer (10x Genomics). Counted the nuclei using a haemocytometer and diluted to the concentration of 3,220 nuclei/μL. Further processes were according to the 10x Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression Reagent Kits user guide. We typically aimed to 10,000 nuclei/sample/reaction. Libraries from individual samples were pooled and sequenced on the NovaSeq X-25B sequencing system, which were performed by Annoroad Gene Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd, Beijing.

Spinal cord tissue preparation for Stereo-seq
Snap-frozen spinal cord samples were embedded into OCT (4583#, Sakura) and quickly froze them with dry ice. After the freezing, spinal cord tissue blocks were stored in -80°C before the slicing. All the instruments were cleaned with RNase Zap (AM9780, Invitrogen) to minimize the RNA degradation. 10 μm cryosections were obtained to perform Stereo-seq. Before Stereo-seq experiment procedure, we assessed RNA quality of the samples, only samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) value ≥ 7 were used for Stereo-seq experiment. Spinal cord cryosections were laid on Stereo-seq chips for mRNA collection and followed by gene sequencing on the DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing system. These were performed by Annoroad Gene Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd, Beijing.

In vivo chemotaxis assays
Timed-pregnant mice at embryonic day (e)13.5 were anaesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 10% Nembutal. A midline skin incision followed by a laparotomy was made, the uterus was exteriorized, and individual embryos were injected in utero into the spinal cord central canal with 5 ng of the SSTR2 antagonist CYN154806 (dissolved in sterile 1× PBS); control embryos received equal volumes of 1× PBS. The uterus was gently returned to the abdominal cavity and the abdominal wall and skin were closed with interrupted sutures. Embryos were harvested at e15.5 (48 h post‑injection), washed in ice‑cold 1× PBS, embedded in OCT compound, flash‑frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C. 10 μm cryosections were prepared and RNAscope was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (ACD, CA, USA). Images were acquired on a Leica STED confocal microscope and processed using Fiji.

Differential expression and GO analysis
Differential expression analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test implemented in Scanpy (v1.10.1) 96. Statistically significant marker genes for each group were defined by a log[FC] > 0.25 and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P-value < 0.01. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the enrichR (v3.4) 97, leveraging the updated GO Biological Process 2025 and GO Molecular Function 2025 libraries. For each group, the top 100 marker genes, ranked by log[FC] in descending order, were used as the input gene list. The statistical significance of the overlap between the input genes and the library gene sets was calculated using Fisher’s exact test. Terms from both the Biological Process and Molecular Function categories were integrated and prioritized based on their adjusted P-values for subsequent functional interpretation.

Identification of cCREs and differentially accessible regions
To delineate the regulatory landscape, candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) were identified by performing pseudo-bulk peak calling within each cell type using MACS3 98 with a q-value threshold of 0.01. High-noise regions overlapping with the hg38 blacklist (v2) 99 were excluded, and group-specific peak sets were merged into a non-overlapping consensus matrix to establish a unified set of genomic features across the dataset. Differentially accessible regions (DARs) for each lineage were subsequently identified using the marker_regions function in SnapATAC2 (v2.6.4) 100, with statistical significance determined via a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 0.05). 

Motif enrichment analysis 
TF motif enrichment analysis was performed using HOMER 101 findMotifsGenome.pl with the hg38 reference genome. We utilized the -size given parameter to scan the full peak regions, comparing target sequences against a custom background of shuffled genomic regions. Statistical significance was determined via a cumulative hypergeometric test. Enrichment scores (-log10 P) were used to prioritize candidate regulators across cell types.

Mapping cell types from snRNA-seq onto Stereo-seq/
Integration of snRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics data
For each developmental stage, Stereo-seq (bin30) and stage-matched snRNA-seq datasets were integrated using Seurat (v5.0.1) 102. Both datasets were independently processed using SCTransform for normalization and variance stabilization. Integration anchors were identified through Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) via the FindIntegrationAnchors function (dims = 1:30), followed by dataset merging using IntegrateData. After computing principal components on the integrated matrix, the Harmony 103 algorithm was applied to the PCA space to mitigate platform-specific batch effects and generate a joint embedding. Cell-type identities were transferred from the snRNA-seq reference to spatial bins using a customized k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) mapping strategy. For each spatial bin, k=50 nearest neighbors were identified within the joint Harmony manifold. For bins exhibiting limited direct snRNA-seq neighbors, the candidate pool was expanded by incorporating the snRNA-seq neighbor information from adjacent spatial bins. Final cell-type assignments were determined based on the majority vote (mode) of the top 10 prioritized snRNA-seq neighbors. This stage-matched integration pipeline was applied independently across all developmental timepoints to ensure high-resolution anatomical mapping of cell lineages while preserving stage-specific biological heterogeneity.

Spatial Mapping of Cell Types using Cell2location
To resolve the spatial distribution of cell types across human spinal cord development, we employed cell2location (v0.1.4) 104 to integrate stage-matched snRNA-seq references with Stereo-seq data independently for each developmental stage. Briefly, reference cell-type signatures were estimated using a Negative Binomial regression model from snRNA-seq data after filtering for rare cell types (<10 nuclei) and low-expressed genes. These signatures were then mapped onto the spatial transcriptomics data using the Cell2location model. We set the expected average cell abundance per location (cells_per_location) to 3, while other hyperparameters followed default recommendations. The model was trained for 10,000 epochs, and the 5% quantile of the posterior distribution (q05) was exported as a confident measure of cell-type abundance at each spatial location for downstream analysis.

Definition of neurogenesis and gliogenesis related gene set
A neurogenesis-related gene set was defined based on prior literature. Genes were selected if they have been experimentally demonstrated to play a direct role in neurogenesis, including the regulation of neural stem/progenitor cell maintenance, fate specification, neuronal differentiation, migration, or early neuronal maturation. Literature curation was performed manually, focusing on genes with well-established functional evidence in mammalian neurodevelopment.
The final neurogenesis-related gene set consisted of the following genes: SOX2 105,106, SOX2-OT107, HES1/HES5 108,109, GLI2/GLI3110,111, TCF4 112,113, NPAS3114,115, NFIA116, NFIB117, PAX3118,119, QKI120–122, MSI2123, MIR99AHG124, PTPRZ1125,126, NTRK2127,128, ERBB4129,130, CTNNA3, PARD3, PARD3B, EZH2131, and HMGB2132,133.
As same, a gliogenesis-related gene set was manually curated based on prior literature. Genes were included if they have been experimentally demonstrated to regulate glial fate specification, oligodendrocyte progenitor cell proliferation, or glial maturation and myelination. The final gene set included OLIG2134–136, SOX10137,138, SOX8139,140, NKX2-2141,142, MYRF143,144, PDGFRA145, MBP146,147, BCAS1148, GAL3ST1149, FA2H150, UGT8151,152, PMP2153, PLP1154,155, GPR17156,157, TCF7L2158,159, ERBB3160, and NFASC161.
These curated gene sets were subsequently used as predefined pathways for downstream enrichment and functional analyses.

Spatial transcription factor regulatory analysis
To resolve the spatial landscape of TF activity, we integrated stage-matched snRNA-seq, snATAC-seq, and Stereo-seq data. Leveraging the 10x Genomics Multiome platform, where transcriptomes and chromatin accessibility are captured within the same nuclei sharing identical barcodes, we established a tripartite mapping (snATAC–snRNA–Spatial).
First, snRNA-seq profiles were anchored to Stereo-seq (bin30) spots using the manifold-alignment and k-NN mapping strategy described above. Because of the shared barcodes, the corresponding snATAC-seq profiles were directly projected onto the spatial coordinates of their paired snRNA-seq counterparts. To maintain the anatomical integrity of the Stereo-seq grid, a spatially reconstructed ATAC peak-count matrix was generated for each stage, where spots without mapped nuclei were assigned zero counts. To mitigate the inherent sparsity of the chromatin accessibility data, we applied MAGIC 162 (solver = approximate) to the reconstructed spatial ATAC matrix for data imputation.
Spatially resolved TF regulatory activity was quantified using chromVAR 32 implemented in the GPU-accelerated scPrinter (v1.2.1.dev10+g5ecaa1414) framework 163. Background peaks were sampled based on GC content and accessibility frequency using the sample_bg_peaks function (250 iterations). Motif scanning was performed against the FigR Human Motifs database 164 using the hg38 reference genome with a p-value threshold of 5e-5. Finally, motif deviation scores were calculated to represent the relative TF activity at each spatial coordinate across the samples.

Gene regulatory network analysis
To resolve the genomic regulatory landscape at single-cell resolution, we performed topic modeling and enhancer-driven gene regulatory network (eGRN) inference using pycisTopic (v2.0a0) 165 and SCENIC+ (v1.0a1) 35. Initially, fragment matrices were extracted from the SnapATAC2-processed peak counts and modeled using Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) via the MALLET implementation by pycisTopic. The optimal number of topics (50) was determined based on log-likelihood metrics, and characteristic open chromatin regions were identified using Otsu thresholding and top-region (n=3,000) binarization strategies. Subsequently, we integrated snMultiome data through the SCENIC+ pipeline to construct eGRNs. pycisTarget and differential enrichment of motifs (DEM) were then implemented to determine whether the candidate CREs were linked to a given TF 166. Next, eRegulons, defined as TF-region-gene triplets, were then inferred, encompassing TFs, their motif-enriched regions, and the associated target genes were provided by SCENIC+ using the default settings. For each retained eRegulon, specificity scores were calculated using the RSS algorithm based on region- or gene-based eRegulon enrichment scores (AUC scores) 35. To characterize the core regulatory circuitry, we reconstructed TF-region-target gene triplets using the create_nx_tables framework. These networks were further refined by incorporating cell type-specific information, including differentially expressed genes (DEGs), differentially accessible regions (DARs), and pseudobulk expression/accessibility profiles (aggregated by cell type). The finalized weighted networks, prioritizing high-confidence triplets based on TF-to-region and region-to-gene importance scores, were exported and visualized using Cytoscape 167 to delineate the cell type-specific regulatory architectures.

RNA velocity and latent time
To infer the directional trajectories and developmental potential of developmental lineages, we performed RNA velocity analysis using the scVelo Python toolkit (v0.3.2) 33,34. Spliced and unspliced count matrices were generated and merged with the pre-processed AnnData object. The dataset was filtered and normalized using the pp.filter_and_normalize function, retaining genes with at least 10 shared counts between spliced and unspliced layers, and selecting the top 5,000 highly variable genes. Moments were then computed using the pp.moments function with default parameters. For robust estimation of cell dynamics, we initially employed the stochastic model to calculate velocity vectors. Subsequently, we implemented the dynamical model by executing tl.recover_dynamics to solve the full transcriptional kinetic equations for each gene. Following the recovery of kinetic parameters, cell-wise velocities were calculated using mode='dynamical', and a velocity graph was constructed via tl.velocity_graph. We utilized latent time (tl.latent_time) to represent the internal transcriptional clock of the cells. Potential root and terminal states were identified using the tl.terminal_states function. To identify key drivers of lineage transitions, we employed tl.rank_dynamical_genes to rank genes based on their fit likelihood and dynamic significance across different groups. Finally, velocity streams and latent time gradients were projected onto the UMAP embedding for visualization.

Multiscale footprinting and inference of TF binding
To infer TF occupancy and chromatin footprints at base-pair resolution, we applied the scPrinter framework (v1.2.1.dev10+g5ecaa1414) together with the PRINT/seq2PRINT models 39. Single-nucleus ATAC–seq fragments were processed using scPrinter. After quality control, peaks were called using the seq2PRINT preset to define training regions for multiscale footprint inference. Chromatin accessibility was summarized into a peak-by-cell matrix, and TF activity was estimated using chromVAR motif deviation scores implemented in scPrinter with default parameters.
Single cells were aggregated into pseudo-bulk profiles for footprint analysis. Pseudo-bulk centers were ordered along the inferred latent-time trajectory. For each center, neighboring cells in latent-time space were iteratively added until each pseudo-bulk reached approximately five million ATAC–seq fragments. Pseudo-bulks were constructed independently within each annotated cell type.
A base seq2PRINT model was initially trained on chromatin accessibility data aggregated across all cells. This pretrained model was subsequently fine-tuned for individual pseudo-bulks using low-rank adaptation (LoRA). Using these pseudo-bulk-specific models, we performed multiscale footprinting to resolve the binding of proteins across window sizes ranging from 4–200 bp. The TF binding scores (single-base resolution) were used to predict specific TF occupancy by integrating sequence-level information with footprint features. Simultaneously, footprint scores at varying scales were utilized to characterize the physical protection of DNA by proteins. While smaller window footprints reflect individual TF binding, footprint scores evaluated at a 140-bp scale were specifically utilized to infer nucleosome positioning and occupancy. By integrating these multiscale signals, we characterized the detailed intra-CRE architecture, including TF recruitment and the associated phasing or displacement of flanking nucleosomes across developmental stages. To annotate candidate TF binding sites, genomic sequences corresponding to the CREs were scanned for TF motifs using the JASPAR2022 core motif database 168. Motif scanning was performed with a pseudocount of 1e-4, a p-value threshold of 5e-5, and a 7-bp sliding window. The resulting motif instances were mapped onto each CRE, generating a high-resolution reference of candidate binding positions.

In silico transcription factor perturbation
To predict the developmental consequences of TF loss-of-function, we employed the CellOracle (v0.20.0) framework 40 by integrating regulatory priors derived from the SCENIC+ analysis. A high-confidence base gene-regulatory network (GRN) was established by filtering SCENIC+ links based on three stringent criteria: (1) a positive Spearman correlation (ρ>0.03) between chromatin accessibility and target gene expression; (2) an importance score within the top 50th percentile; and (3) a genomic distance between regulatory regions and target genes within 500 kb. Using these priors, we refined cluster-specific GRN models by fitting regularized Ridge regression (α=10) on kNN-imputed snRNA-seq data, retaining the top 2,000 edges per cluster ranked by connection strength. To simulate the perturbation, the expression of candidate TFs was computationally set to zero. The perturbation signal was then propagated through the inferred models using an iterative simulation algorithm with a depth of three iterations (n_propagation=3), accounting for both direct and indirect downstream regulatory effects. The resulting predicted shifts in global gene expression were projected as transition vectors onto the UMAP embedding to visualize the impact of TF perturbation on cell-state transitions and differentiation trajectories.

Identification of Spatial Niches
To define spatially consistent functional domains, we employed NicheCompass (v0.2.3) 44 to construct an interpretable latent space representing cellular "niches" based on spatial transcriptomics data. We integrated gene expression profiles with a prior knowledge Gene Program (GP) mask derived from the OmniPath 169, MEBOCOST 170, and NicheNet 171 databases. Spatial neighborhood graphs for all samples (GW6–GW17) were constructed with k=4 nearest neighbors using the Squidpy (v1.6.1) package 172. The NicheCompass model was trained for 100 epochs, utilizing linear masked decoders to reconstruct gene expression and spatial adjacencies into biologically defined GPs. Spatial niches were subsequently identified by performing Leiden clustering on the resulting latent neighbor graph at a resolution of 0.36. These niches were manually annotated based on their anatomical locations.

Neighbourhood enrichment and intercellular communication modelling
To characterize the spatial architecture and local cellular dependencies, we performed neighborhood enrichment and communication modeling within each individual sample. We first quantified the spatial proximity of cell types by calculating neighborhood enrichment counts for each sample independently using the nhood_enrichment function from Squidpy (v.1.6.1) 172. Since our data consists of spot-level spatial transcriptomics, we utilized Cell2location (v0.1.4) 104 to estimate the abundance of specific cell types within each spot. The resulting cell-type compositions served as the basis for downstream communication modeling.
Intercellular communication was modeled using the graph neural-network-based NCEM (v0.1.4) method 51. Specifically, we implemented a node-centric linear expression model to predict gene expression states as a function of both the local cell-type composition and the surrounding niche environment. The dependencies between sender and receiver identities were constrained by a spatial connectivity graph derived from the local neighborhood of each spot. Significant spatial interactions were identified based on two criteria: (1) a magnitude of interaction (the Euclidean norm of coefficients in the node-centric model) exceeding 0.5, and (2) at least 50 differentially expressed genes (q < 0.05 for specific sender–receiver interaction terms) detected. For visualization, only significant communication links were represented in circular plots.

Quantification of ligand–receptor communication
To complement the unsupervised spatial modeling and identify specific molecular mediators of intercellular signaling, we implemented CellChat (v.2.1.2) for targeted ligand–receptor (L–R) communication analysis 173. This supervised approach allowed for the precise quantification of interaction strengths based on curated biological knowledge. We integrated gene expression profiles with spatial coordinates and utilized the CellChatDB.human database to calculate cell-type-specific communication probabilities via the truncatedMean (trim = 0.1) method. To reflect the spatial architecture of the tissue, we incorporated distance-based constraints: contact-dependent signaling was restricted to a contact.range of 50, while diffusible signaling was modeled with an interaction.range of 100, ensuring that communication probabilities were weighted by the physical proximity of sender and receiver groups. The resulting networks were summarized at the signaling pathway level. To highlight the robust biological signals, we applied a stringent filtering strategy, retaining only interactions with P < 0.05 and subsequently applying a top 30% quantile filter to the total interaction strength per pathway. The final weighted directed graphs were visualized using chord, bubble, and heatmap plots to exhibit the interaction strength and signaling frequency among cell types.

Spatially resolved trait mapping and GWAS enrichment
To identify the spatial distribution of cells associated with human complex traits, we applied gsMap (v1.73.5) 91 to integrate GW17 Stereo-seq data with GWAS summary statistics. We first learned the latent representations of spatial spots via run_find_latent_representations and calculated Gene Specificity Scores (GSS) through run_latent_to_gene by aggregating information from 30 homogeneous neighbors and 50 spatial neighbors. Following the generation of stratified LD scores via run_generate_ldscore using the enhancer_first strategy, we performed spatial LD score regression via run_spatial_ldsc to estimate spot-level heritability enrichment. To evaluate genetic associations at the tissue level, we applied the Cauchy combination test via run_cauchy_combination, which aggregated spot-level p-values based on our pre-defined spatial niche annotations. Finally, trait-associated spatial enrichment patterns were resolved and visualized through run_report.
To evaluate the genetic relevance of diverse cell types in the developing spinal cord to human complex traits, we performed cell-type-specific linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC, v.1.0.1) 174 following standard protocols. For each cell type, the differentially accessible regions (DARs) identified via SnapATAC2 (as described above) were utilized as binary annotations. The superset of all cCREs was employed as the background control.
GWAS summary statistics for various neuropsychiatric, neurodegenerative, and complex traits were primarily sourced from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC) (https://pgc.unc.edu/) CTGlab (https://cncr.nl/ctg/) and the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). Detailed information regarding the GWAS datasets used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table 1.
